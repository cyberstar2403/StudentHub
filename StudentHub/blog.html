LEARNING STRATEGY · 18 October 2026

Why active recall beats another hour of rereading

A practical explanation of retrieval practice, why effort matters, and how to use short self-tests without turning every study session into an exam.

Active recall means trying to retrieve information from memory before checking your notes. It can feel harder than rereading, but that effort helps reveal what you know and what still needs work. Start small: after reading a section, close the book and write three key ideas. Then check the source and correct gaps. Repeat the process a day later, and again later in the week. Use flashcards for facts, blank-page summaries for concepts, and practice questions for problem-solving subjects. The important part is feedback: retrieval without checking can reinforce mistakes. Keep sessions short enough to repeat consistently.

STUDENT LIFE · 12 October 2026

A calmer way to plan a busy semester

Replace the impossible perfect timetable with a flexible weekly system that accounts for classes, deadlines, rest, and unexpected work.

A useful semester plan begins with reality, not ambition. Put fixed commitments into a calendar first: classes, work, travel, and rest. Then list deadlines and work backwards to identify the next action. Instead of writing “study biology”, write “review lecture 4 and answer five questions”. Assign focused blocks that fit your energy and leave space between demanding tasks. At the end of each week, check what moved forward and what needs to change. A plan should reduce decision fatigue, not become another source of guilt.

COMPUTER SCIENCE · 04 October 2026

How to approach a programming problem

A simple framework for understanding requirements, planning an algorithm, testing edge cases, and improving your solution.

Before writing code, restate the problem in your own words. Identify the inputs, expected output, and constraints. Work through a small example by hand, then describe the steps in plain language or pseudocode. Once the logic is clear, translate it into code in small pieces. Test a normal case, a boundary case, and an unexpected input. If the result is wrong, inspect one step at a time rather than rewriting everything. Good programming is not typing quickly; it is making reasoning visible and checking it carefully.

ACADEMIC SKILLS · 26 September 2026

Take notes you can actually use

A note-taking approach that captures meaning during class and turns it into a reliable revision resource afterwards.

Notes are not a transcript. During a lecture, prioritise definitions, relationships, examples, and questions. Leave space to fill gaps later. Soon after class, organise the material into a short outline and write a few questions that test the main ideas. When revising, answer those questions before looking at the notes. If a page is difficult to understand a week later, improve its structure rather than simply adding more words. The best notes help you think, retrieve, and connect ideas.
